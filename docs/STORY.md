# Master of None: Story and Systems Bible v0.2

Working document. Everything here is a proposal, not a decision. Argue with it.

---

## 1. The spine

The full proverb is the game's structure:

> Jack of all trades, master of none, but oftentimes better than a master of one.

Each chapter is a piece of it. That gives you a title system that already means something before the player understands why.

| Ch | Title | Age | Core question |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | **Still Water** | 9 to 16 | What does he owe the people who made him? |
| 1 | **Measure Twice** | 16 to 18 | Can he be taught? |
| 2 | **Cut Once** | 18 to 19 | What survives when the teacher is gone? |
| 3 | **Piecework** | 19 to 21 | Can he exist without papers? |
| 4 | **Jack of All Trades** | 21 to 23 | Is breadth a strength or an excuse? |
| 5 | **Master of None** | 23 to 24 | What did breadth cost him? |
| 6+ | **Better Than a Master of One** | 24+ | Post-launch / sequel territory |

"Measure twice, cut once" split across chapters 1 and 2 is the load-bearing idea. Chapter 1 is Fen teaching him patience. Chapter 2 is the cut he cannot take back. Players will only feel the pairing on reflection, which is exactly right.

**Title screen:** do not put "Master of None" on it as a chapter marker. The game is called Master of None. When Chapter 5 loads and the card reads "Master of None," the player should get a small cold drop, because they realise the game has been named after his failure the whole time. If you title-drop early you spend that for nothing.

## 2. Naming

### The protagonist

Both genders share one nickname so you write one script, one save format, one set of dialogue lines.

- **Boy: Jacob, called Jack.**
- **Girl: Jacqueline, called Jack.**

The nickname is symmetrical, which makes the pronoun and name handling clean: one token, two values, no branching dialogue.

**Nobody in the game uses the full name except the mother.** She says it exactly twice: once in Chapter 0, once in Chapter 5. Everyone else, including Jack himself, says Jack. When Fen learns his name in Chapter 1, Jack gives the short version, and Fen never asks for the long one. That is the whole gender-difference budget and it costs two lines.

No surname for a long time. When Jack finally has to give one on a form in Chapter 3, he makes one up on the spot. That is a scene, and the surname he invents should be something the player recognises from Loden.

### The cats

**Mother: Marigold.** He names her the day he finds her, at nine. He names her after the marigolds he grows in tins along his doorframe, because they are the only thing he has ever successfully kept alive and because they are the only thing in that house he chose.

He never says the name where his parents can hear it. Saying it out loud in the house would have made her real to them, and everything that becomes real to them gets ruined. So for seven years it is a name he only uses in the warehouse.

**This closes the loop on the flowers.** He grew marigolds because his room has no door and the lake breeds mosquitoes, and marigolds keep them down. He named the cat after them. Then he lays them on her grave. Nothing in that chain was designed backwards and none of it needs a line of dialogue to explain it.

**Surviving calico kitten: Mari.** He cannot say the full name without stopping, so he cuts it short. He tells himself it is a different name. It is not.

The **first word Jack ever speaks out loud**, at nine, alone in the warehouse, is "marigold." Not a plea, not a word to a parent. A name he made up for something he chose. Build the entire scene around that one word.

The other three get names on the grave marker too, scratched into wood with a nail. Let the player type them. Whatever they type shows up on that marker for the rest of the game, and once more in Chapter 5. That is your cheapest and most devastating personalisation hook.

### Cast and place names

Deliberately unplaced. Not Japan, not France, not America. A coastal country that does not exist, using euros because the currency is a stand-in and nobody will question it.

**Chapter 1 town: Loden.** Inland, timber, one road out, a river. Peaceful in the Doraemon sense: small, warm, quietly indifferent.

**Chapter 3+ city: Vantry.** Port. Ferries, container yards, three bad neighbourhoods and one that is pretending.

| Name | Role | First appears |
|---|---|---|
| Nikolaj Fen ("Fen") | The old carpenter. Cruel on the surface, methodical underneath. Widower. | Ch 1 |
| Marisse Fen | Fen's estranged daughter. Not a villain. Arrives with grief and paperwork. | Ch 2 |
| Sumi Ando | Runs a bread and noodle counter on Loden's main street. Feeds Jack for free twice, then never again, because she is not running a charity and says so. | Ch 1 |
| Oyelaran | Runs the Loden press. Gives Jack the paper route. Pays cash, asks nothing. | Ch 1 |
| Wren | Bike courier, Jack's age, met in Vantry. First actual friend. Not a love interest by default. | Ch 3 |
| Tibs | Twelve years old, follows Jack around, wants to be him. The reason Chapter 5 hurts. | Ch 3 |
| Sgt. Duras | Loden's corrupt constable. | Ch 1 |
| Corbin Hask ("Nickel") | Mid-level in the delivery ring. Survives Chapter 1. Resurfaces in Vantry. | Ch 1 |
| Insp. Ilva Rask | Regional investigator. Not a saviour. Uses Jack. | Ch 1 |
| Osgood Rell | Master joiner in Vantry. Technically better than Jack at one thing and worse at everything else, including being alive. The "master of one" foil. | Ch 4 |

## 3. Chapter 0: Still Water

Everything through the lake. Playable, not a cutscene. Roughly 60 to 90 minutes now that the vignettes are real.

### 3.1 The room with no door

Jack's room is on the **ground floor, facing the lake**. It has a doorframe and no door. His parents never hung one, on the stated grounds that he has nothing to hide and therefore needs no privacy.

This single detail does more work than any dialogue could, and it should be established in the first ninety seconds without anyone remarking on it:

- He hears every argument in the house, all of them, every night. There is no version of his childhood where he gets to not hear it.
- Every argument can hear him. He learns to be silent as a survival behaviour, which is the mechanical and emotional origin of the mutism.
- **It causes the tragedy.** A room with no door cannot hide four kittens. The chain runs: no privacy, no door, the kittens are heard, the father takes them. Nothing about the ending is arbitrary once this is established.

**The only door Jack ever closes is the front door of the house, from the outside, at the end of Chapter 0.** Give that a sound. Give it the only real sound in the scene.

### 3.2 The marigolds

The lake is close, the room is unscreened, and the mosquitoes are constant. At some point before the game starts, Jack put marigolds in tins along the doorframe and the window, because marigolds keep mosquitoes down.

It worked. It is the first thing he ever did well, the first thing he ever did for himself, and the only living colour in the house's palette.

Gardening becomes a small ongoing activity across Chapter 0: watering, repotting, moving tins for light, replacing one that dies. Low stakes, quiet, repeatable. It is also skill node zero and it establishes the game's core verb, which is that Jack fixes and tends things nobody asked him to.

The player should never be told that the cat is named after the flowers. They will work it out at the grave, and they will work it out at exactly the wrong moment.

### 3.3 Age 9, the hinge year

Three things happen in one year and the game should compress them so the player feels them as one event.

**The jeep.** The last thing his parents ever gave him was a remote control jeep, when he was four. It stopped working when he was five. It has been in a wooden box under his bed for four years, along with a **blue pen with a small plastic fan on the cap that spins when you blow on it**, and whatever else the player finds in there.

At nine he takes the jeep out just to look at it. Curiosity takes over and he opens it.

*Loophole fix, and it improves the scene:* do not make it a motherboard. A nine year old cannot debug a motherboard and the player will not believe it. Make it **corroded battery contacts and one wire pulled loose from the motor terminal**. He scrapes the green off the contacts with the edge of a coin and twists the wire back onto the post. He has no idea that is what he is doing or why it works. It works.

He drives it across the kitchen floor for the better part of an hour, in the same room as both his parents. Nobody looks up. Nobody notices it works. Nobody notices it is the last thing they ever gave him.

*The reason the gifts stopped at four is that four is roughly when it became clear he was not going to speak.* Jack has spotted that correlation himself and drawn the wrong conclusion from it: he thinks they stopped because something was wrong with him. They stopped because their marriage did. **Never state either out loud.** Put the timing on screen and let the player assemble it.

Mechanically this is the tutorial for the entire trade system. Repair goes to 1. The skill screen opens for the first time and already reads `repair: 1 / mastery: requires certification`. The joke of the game is running before the player knows there is one.

**Finding Marigold.** A playable quest. The family owns a disused warehouse at the lake edge, which is how he has access to it and why nobody thinks about it. He finds a young calico there. He carries her home and asks.

The answer is no. It is not discussed, negotiated, or explained. It is a no delivered without either parent putting down what they are holding.

So he keeps her in the warehouse instead. For seven years. He feeds her from his own plate, which means the player is choosing to eat less from the very first chapter, and he builds her shelter out of pallets, badly at first and then less badly. **That is where carpentry starts.** By the time he meets Fen he has seven years of untaught, unwitnessed practice behind him, which is why Fen keeps him.

**Speaking.** One afternoon, alone in the warehouse, he says her name out loud. That is the first word of his life. See section 2.

The empty dialogue boxes fill in from that point on. Nobody in the house treats it as a good day. The mother cries about it in a way that is about herself. The father says something about how it took long enough.

### 3.4 Ages 10 to 15, the montage

Seasonal vignettes, three to five minutes each, manga-page framing with one interactive beat. Suggested set:

- The warehouse shelter getting better each year, four versions of the same structure
- Fishing off the lake edge, which is where the food system is taught
- The marigolds, one full cycle, including one year they fail
- The paper map, folded and refolded until the creases go soft
- Sunsets from the ground floor window, at the lake, which is the sit animation's home
- **The water line.** Every seasonal vignette, the swamp at the warehouse edge is a little further in than last time. Never mention it. The player will notice on the fourth one and by then it is dread.

### 3.5 Age 16, the kittens

**The birth.** Marigold gives birth on Jack's lap in the warehouse. Four kittens. She trusted him that much and the game should not underline it. Manga-page interstitial, minimal text, no music.

**Eight weeks in the warehouse.** This is the best sequence in Chapter 0 and it should be genuinely joyful, because everything after it is not. The kittens open their eyes. They get names. They learn to climb the pallets he built. Jack is happier than he is at any other point in the game, and the player gets to just be there.

**Why eight weeks matters, and this closes the biggest hole in the new material:** kittens that are two days old cannot survive being carried away by a runaway teenager, and Chapter 1 collapses if Jack has to bottle feed every three hours while standing in a carpenter's doorway. At eight to nine weeks they are weaned and eating solids. Mari can be carried in a bag and Chapter 1 works.

**The flood forces the move.** Autumn rain. The lake edge comes up through the warehouse floor and the pallets Jack built are standing in water. This is the payoff for the rising water line in the montage. He has no choice, and the player has watched the reason arrive for six vignettes.

He moves them into the cardboard box under his bed. In a room with no door.

**Two days.** That is all it takes, and two days is generous. On the second night one of them cries.

**The day.** The father finds them. The fight is about bad luck and about money and about none of those things. Jack retreats to a room he physically cannot close, which is the worst place in the world to be losing an argument.

**The lake, at dusk.** Jack is at ground level now, not upstairs, so the distance is horizontal instead of vertical. **The father walks to the far bank.** Jack sees it across the water from his window, in silhouette, against the sunset.

The production note stands and is now stronger: silhouette, distance, water audio only, no music, no impact, no bodies. Jack runs along the bank rather than down a flight of stairs, which means he can see the whole way and it is still too late. Do not shorten that run. Do not let the player influence it.

One kitten survives.

### 3.6 The box

Before he goes, he is in his room, and the wooden box is under the bed.

**Let the player choose what to take.** Three slots. Candidates: the jeep, the blue pen, a fishing line, a marigold seedling in a tin, the paper map (always taken, not optional), a photograph. Whatever the player takes is in the toolbox in Chapter 4 and on the table in Chapter 5.

The blue pen is what Jack writes on the map with for the whole of Chapter 0. In Chapter 1, Fen gives him a carpenter's pencil, and the handwriting on the map visibly changes from that point. If the player left the pen behind, the change happens anyway and it means something slightly different.

The run out of the house is the last thing you control in Chapter 0. No fade. It cuts on him crossing the town line, holding one kitten inside his jacket, having closed the only door he ever gets to close.

## 4. Chapter 1: Measure Twice

**Opening beat:** he buries them at the treeline outside Loden, before he has any idea where he is going. Marker, four names, flowers. He names Marigold here. He renames the survivor here. Then he walks into a town he has never seen with a kitten, a paper map that does not cover this area, and no money.

**Fen.** The two-hour silence is your best scene in the chapter, so build it as a mechanic rather than a cutscene. Jack stands in the shop doorway. The only interaction available is one dialogue option: *"i can work."* Fen tells him to leave. Wait. Ask again. Wait. The sun moves across the floor in real time compressed to about six minutes. Fen keeps working and stops responding entirely after the fourth attempt. On the ninth attempt, with the light gone orange, he says "sweep." That is the whole hiring scene and the player will remember it for years.

**The room under the stairs.** Yes, keep it. Do not lampshade the Harry Potter reference in dialogue, let it sit. Furnish it over the chapter: a crate, then a shelf he builds badly, then a shelf he builds well. The room is the visible skill tree.

**Two years, compressed.** Interstitial vignettes between quests, styled as manga-ish still pages with one interactive beat each. Suggested set of eight:
- Learning to hold a chisel by watching, getting it wrong, bleeding.
- Feeding Mari, first time, on money he earned.
- Fen wordlessly correcting a joint at 5am while Jack sleeps.
- Winter, the shop heater dying, both of them working in coats.
- Sumi Ando cutting him off from free food.
- His first finished piece, a stool, which Fen sells and does not tell him about.
- Fen's hands starting to shake.
- A missing-persons poster with his face on it, in the Loden post office. He takes it down. He does not throw it away.

**The paper route.** Wakes at 4:40. This is where the world opens. Sunrise, empty streets, the same nine faces every morning, and crucially: it is how Jack learns to read a town economically. Who has money, who is pretending, who gets deliveries at odd hours. Make this a real system, because it is the mechanical seed of everything he does in Chapters 3 and 4. Each morning route can surface one observation that unlocks a job lead.

**The delivery arc.** Keep it, but fix the ending. Current version resolves too cleanly.

Revised: Jack takes sealed boxes for cash from Hask. Four or five runs. The reveal is not dramatic; a box breaks. He goes to Duras because Duras is the only authority he knows. Duras is on the payroll and tells Hask. The beating happens off-screen, again in silhouette, and Jack wakes in Loden's small clinic.

Rask arrives, but she is not there for him. She has been building a case on the route for eight months and Jack is a useful data point, not a rescue target. She takes his statement. She notices he has no ID. She does not report it, which is a favour, and she makes sure he understands it is a favour. Duras is suspended. One low-level courier goes down. Hask walks, because Hask is not the top and the top is not in Loden.

That gives you: a moral world that is not a fairy tale, an antagonist who survives to Chapter 3, and an authority figure who is neither corrupt nor kind, just occupied. Rask's card stays in Jack's inventory permanently. He can call it exactly once in the whole game and you should make him regret whichever choice he makes.

**Fen's lecture.** Not a speech about drugs. A speech about the difference between taking a risk and taking a shortcut, delivered while measuring a board. He is furious in the specific way of an old man who was not planning to care about anyone again.

**Chapter 1 ends** with Jack, 18, running the shop's daily work while Fen watches from a chair.

## 5. Chapter 2: Cut Once

This is the chapter you asked most about, so here is the full shape.

### Act 1: The overload (the point of the chapter)

Fen cannot work. Jack now runs three things at once and the game deliberately gives you more than you can hold:

- The shop: commissions with deadlines, materials to buy, a ledger.
- Fen: medication at fixed times, meals, getting him to appointments in the next town over.
- The paper route: still the only reliable cash.

Make the calendar visibly impossible. Two of these three can be done well on any given day. Never three. The player will start dropping the paper route, or start missing medication times, and either way they will feel guilty about a decision the game forced. That guilt is the chapter's entire emotional payload and it is worth more than any cutscene.

### Act 2: The morning the shop does not open

No death scene. No music sting. Jack finishes the route, comes back, and the shop is dark and the shutter is down, which has never happened. Interact with the door. Interact with the door again. Go upstairs.

Then a full in-game day where you can do nothing but ordinary things. No quests available. The shops are open. Sumi will still sell you bread. Nobody in Loden knows yet. Let the player walk around a town that has not changed while the only thing that mattered has.

### Act 3: Marisse

The funeral is small. Marisse Fen arrives from Vantry, thirty-eight, has not spoken to her father in eleven years, and is not cruel. She is grieving somebody she was not finished being angry at, and she finds an unpapered teenager living under the stairs.

**This is where you fix the biggest hole in the outline.** The shop does not fail because Jack is bad at business. He is good at it. The shop fails because Jack cannot legally inherit, cannot legally run, and cannot legally have been employed there in the first place. He has no birth certificate, no ID, no tax number, no lease, and no standing whatsoever. Marisse inherits by default. She does not want the shop; she wants it gone. That is not villainy, that is a woman who lives four hours away with her own life.

That single fix makes the entire premise of the rest of the game work: Jack's problem is never competence, it is legitimacy. It also retroactively explains why nobody would hire him at 18 in Chapter 1, which currently reads as "no education" and is not quite enough.

### Act 4: The grace period

Marisse gives Jack forty days to close out the shop's outstanding commissions so the customers are not left hanging, and to clear out. This is the chapter's gameplay:

- A ledger of eight to twelve unfinished jobs, each with a deadline, a materials cost, and a skill requirement.
- Jack cannot finish all of them. Some require Fen's hands.
- Some customers will take a partial refund. Some will not. Some will pay more than owed because they liked the old man.
- Money earned here is the Chapter 3 starting balance, and the player should feel that they set that number themselves.

Add one negotiation thread: Marisse softens slightly, not into affection, into recognition. She finds Fen's records and sees two years of cash entries labelled with Jack's name. She had assumed Jack was taking advantage. She was wrong and she says so, badly, once.

### Act 5: The inheritance

The will leaves Jack nothing, because Fen never made one. But there is a note in the toolbox, four words long, and the toolbox itself, which Marisse hands over because it is the only thing in the building she is certain her father would have wanted Jack to have.

**The toolbox becomes the permanent carrier of your crafting system.** Mechanically, it is your inventory upgrade and your skill anchor for the rest of the game. Narratively, it is the only object Jack owns for the next five years. When things get bad in Chapter 4, "sell the toolbox" should appear as a real, available, terrible option.

### Act 6: Out

Night bus to Vantry. Toolbox, a bag of cash, the paper map with a new town drawn on it in his own handwriting, and Mari in the bag. The last thing you see of Loden is the treeline where the graves are, from the bus window, at dusk.

## 6. Chapters 3 to 5, in outline

### Chapter 3: Piecework (age 19 to 21)

Vantry. Big, indifferent, and every door needs paperwork he does not have.

- **Core loop:** cash-in-hand gig work. Unloading, kitchen prep, courier runs, repairs, market stalls. Each job teaches a trade node.
- **Housing:** a shared room with three strangers, rent due weekly on a hard tick, no negotiation. This is where the upkeep systems get teeth.
- **Wren** enters as a courier who takes pity, then stops pitying and starts respecting. Wren has papers and cannot understand why Jack will not just go get them. That argument is the chapter's best scene.
- **The papers thread starts here.** Getting a legal identity becomes a long, boring, expensive, multi-chapter side quest involving a clerk who is not hostile, just processing a queue. Make bureaucracy the antagonist. It is more honest than a gang.
- **Tibs** appears, a kid working the same jobs illegally, younger than Jack was.
- **Hask resurfaces** in the port, running the same trade at scale. He recognises Jack. He offers again. The player can accept. If they do, Chapter 4 is materially different and materially worse.
- **Chapter ends** when Jack has one job that repeats, and a room with a door that locks.

### Chapter 4: Jack of All Trades (age 21 to 23)

The good years. This is where the game should be genuinely pleasant to play, because Chapter 5 needs the fall.

- Jack takes on a real contract: a small restaurant fit-out, or a building rehabilitation. He can do the carpentry, the basic wiring, the costing, the sourcing, the negotiation and the cooking for the crew. None of it excellently, all of it adequately, and nobody else in the city will do all six for that price.
- **Osgood Rell** is the foil. Better joiner than Jack will ever be. Cannot quote a job, cannot manage a client, cannot bend. Jack wins a contract Rell should have had. Rell is not gracious about it, and he is also not wrong about the quality.
- A small crew forms: Wren, Tibs, one or two others.
- Money arrives. Let the player buy things. A better room, a proper bed, a window that faces the right way for sunsets, a vet appointment for Mari that is preventive rather than emergency. Spending money on comfort should feel enormous after three chapters of scarcity.
- **The crack:** Jack starts taking work slightly beyond what he can actually do, because turning it down means admitting a ceiling. He compensates with hours. The upkeep systems start losing to the calendar again, exactly as in Chapter 2, and the player should recognise the pattern before Jack does.

### Chapter 5: Master of None (age 23 to 24)

- Jack takes a job requiring genuine mastery. Structural, or electrical, something with a real failure mode. He is 80% of the way to competent, which is the most dangerous number in the game.
- **Tibs gets hurt.** Not killed. Hurt in a way that is permanent and Jack's fault, on a job Jack should have subcontracted to Rell and did not, because subcontracting meant admitting the ceiling.
- The title card lands here as an accusation.
- Aftermath: the crew fractures. Wren says the true thing. Jack pays the medical bills, which takes everything, including possibly the toolbox.
- **The map unfolds.** The corner Jack has refused to open since Chapter 0 finally opens. Home stops being clouded.
- **Home is not catharsis.** The house is smaller than he remembered. One parent has died or is ill, the other is alone and diminished and has been telling neighbours a version of events in which they did nothing wrong. There is no forgiveness scene and no confrontation scene. There is a conversation about a broken tap, which Jack fixes, and then he leaves. He goes to the pond. He does not go inside again.
- If the player named the three kittens in Chapter 0, those names appear one more time here. Do not comment on it.
- **Ends** on Jack back in Vantry, starting over, with less than he had in Chapter 4 and more than he had in Chapter 3.

### Chapter 6+: Better Than a Master of One

Post-launch or sequel. Jack teaches. Rell, aging, needs someone who can do the six things he cannot. Tibs, changed, is either estranged or the first apprentice. The papers finally come through and the moment is anticlimactic, which is the point.

## 7. Plot holes and how to close them

| Hole | Fix |
|---|---|
| Nobody looks for a missing 16-year-old | They do. The poster in Chapter 1 is on screen. His parents report him because not reporting looks worse to neighbours, not because they want him back. He avoids everything official from that day forward, which explains the entire papers thread. |
| Why won't anyone hire an intelligent 18-year-old? | Not education. Documentation. He cannot produce ID, so only cash-in-hand work exists for him. This is the game's real antagonist. |
| Why does the shop collapse when Jack is competent? | It does not collapse. He is legally invisible and cannot inherit. Marisse inherits by default. Competence was never the variable. |
| Newborn kittens cannot survive a runaway | Marigold gives birth in the warehouse. The kittens stay there eight to nine weeks, weaned and on solids, before the flood forces the move indoors. Only then does the two day countdown start. Bigger, more readable sprites as a bonus. |
| A nine year old cannot repair a motherboard | He does not. Corroded battery contacts scraped with a coin, and one wire twisted back onto the motor terminal. Genuinely doable by a curious child who does not know what he is doing. |
| Why did the gifts stop when he was four? | Four is when it became clear he was not going to speak. Jack believes the two are connected and that it was his fault. They are connected, but the cause was the marriage. Never say either out loud. |
| Why does he have access to a warehouse? | The family owns it, disused, at the lake edge. That is also why nobody goes there and why the parents never think to look. |
| Hiding four kittens in a room with no door is implausible | Correct, and that is the point. It lasts two days, which is already generous, and the discovery is caused by the same detail that defines his childhood. |
| The pond scene framing breaks now that his room is on the ground floor | Distance becomes horizontal instead of vertical. The father walks to the far bank. Jack watches across water from his window at dusk. Silhouette and water audio unchanged, and the run along the bank is longer and worse than a run down stairs. |
| Fen employing an undocumented minor is illegal | Yes, and he knows it. That is why he pays cash, why he never asks a question, and why he is quietly furious when Jack draws police attention in the delivery arc. It also gives Marisse something real to be angry about. |
| The drug arc resolves too neatly | Rask is investigating, not rescuing. Duras falls, Hask does not. See Chapter 1. |
| A transparent cat bag would suffocate a cat | Mesh panels in the black and blue borders. Two lines of art, one detail that signals you thought about it. Cat people will notice and cat people are your audience. |
| What is the fail state if there are no boss fights? | There is no game over. See section 8. |
| Why can't he go home before Chapter 5? | Mechanically: the map's home corner is folded and the interaction is "unfold." Jack refuses. The refusal line changes each chapter and gets less convincing every time. |

## 8. Systems

**Upkeep, not survival.** Hunger and hydration are modifiers, never a health bar. Low upkeep slows actions, removes the confident dialogue options, and lowers skill-check success. Hitting zero does not kill you. You collapse, you wake in a clinic, and eleven days later a letter arrives.

**Letters.** Physical objects through the door slot or the shop mail. Electricity, water, clinic, tax, and eventually the papers office. You can leave them unopened on the table. They accumulate visibly. Some of them are good news, which is why you cannot just ignore the pile. This is the best pressure system in the design because it is entirely diegetic and costs almost nothing to build.

**Debt.** Compounding but survivable. Debt closes options rather than ending runs. There should be a floor below which the game becomes very hard and very sad but never unwinnable.

**Trades as a web, not a tree.** Carpentry, joinery, wiring, plumbing, cooking, bookkeeping, negotiation, cycling, fishing, repair. Each caps at a level Jack can reach by watching and practising alone. Going past that cap requires a formal apprenticeship, which requires papers, which he does not have. **The stat screen is the theme.** Every skill sits at 70 to 85 and one bar, greyed out, reads "mastery: requires certification."

**Mari.** Not a stat. A presence. She reacts to rooms, follows in some scenes and not others, sits in the window at sunset. She can get sick and generate a vet bill.

**Mari cannot die. Ever. Under any circumstances. This is a hard design rule.** After Chapter 0, a cat death mechanic would break trust with exactly the players who will love this game most, and no amount of thematic justification is worth it. If you want the fear, let her get sick and let the bill hurt.

**The map.** One item, hand-drawn, expanded by Jack in his own handwriting as he explores. Annotations accumulate. The home corner stays folded until Chapter 5.

**Sunsets.** Make them a real mechanic, not decoration. There is a "watch" interaction available at certain spots at certain times. It restores a small amount of something and unlocks a short internal monologue line specific to that chapter. It is free content, it reinforces the theme, and it is the single most screenshot-able thing in the game.

**No combat.** Confirmed. Conflict resolves through dialogue, money, time, or walking away. The beating in Chapter 1 is not a fight scene because Jack does not get a fight scene.

## 9. Art and text direction

- **Perspective:** see the risk note in section 10 before committing to isometric.
- **Internal resolution:** 480x270, integer-scaled to 1920x1080. That is meaningfully larger than Game Boy Pokémon (160x144) and gives you roughly two to three characters plus a house facade on screen, which is what you asked for.
- **Tiles:** 32x32 base. Characters roughly 40 to 48 pixels tall. Big enough for readable faces, small enough to draw.
- **Palette:** hard-limit to 40 to 48 colours across the whole game, weighted warm. Dusk oranges, cold blues for the house and the pond, sawdust yellows for Loden, grey-greens for Vantry. Chapter 0 and Chapter 5 share a palette. Nobody will consciously notice and everybody will feel it.
- **Font:** a 6x8 or 5x7 bitmap face with soft, slightly rounded pixels. Not glitch, not sci-fi, not Press Start 2P. Think worn-in and handwritten rather than digital. Recommend commissioning one custom face; it is cheap and it is the single strongest identity lever you have.
- **All dialogue lowercase.** Confirmed and correct. It reads as interior rather than performed. Exception: keep official documents, letters, signs and forms in mixed case. The contrast between how Jack thinks and how institutions write to him does thematic work for free.
- **Text speed:** slow default, skippable. Silence between lines. Do not fill every beat.

## 10. Tech stack, and the two risks that matter

### Recommended stack

- **Engine: Godot 4.x, GDScript.** Free, no royalties, exports to Windows and macOS from either machine, builds land around 60 to 120 MB for a game this size, best-in-class 2D tooling, and its TileMapLayer handles isometric natively including Y-sorting, which is the thing that usually breaks isometric projects.
- **Dialogue: plain YAML or JSON data files with a small custom runner you write yourself.** Do not use Ink or Dialogic. Ink's Godot integration is community-maintained and has a history of lagging engine releases; Dialogic is good but you will fight its opinions on a script this size. A custom runner over structured data is about 400 lines, and a schema this small is what makes it practical to generate and refactor thousands of dialogue lines programmatically, which is your actual force multiplier.
- **Art: Aseprite** (about 20 EUR, runs on both machines) for sprites. **Tiled** if you want to author maps outside Godot, though Godot's own editor is fine now.
- **Version control: Git, LFS for art.** Non-negotiable on a multi-year project.
- **Save format:** JSON, plaintext, human-readable. Let people mod it. This audience will.

Rejected: Unity (heavier, licensing noise, worse 2D pipeline), GameMaker (Mac dev friction and licence cost), LÖVE (lovely but you build every editor yourself, wrong call for a content-heavy narrative game), web stack with Tauri or Electron (Steam integration and save handling get fragile, and this game's value is in polish).

### Risk 1: isometric will cost you three to five times the art

This is the thing most likely to kill the project, so I want to be direct about it.

Isometric pixel art means every character needs walk cycles in four or eight isometric directions, every prop needs a consistent projection, and every interior needs wall-cutaway logic. For a solo developer writing a forty-hour narrative game while doing an engineering degree, that is not a stylistic choice, it is a second full-time job.

**Recommendation: build it 3/4 top-down instead, the Stardew Valley or Eastward angle.** You get the same "pixel realism" reading, the same warmth, the same readable interiors, and roughly a third of the sprite work. Nobody who plays this game will wish it had been isometric. They will wish it had shipped.

If you keep isometric anyway, hard-limit yourself to four directions, not eight, and design your prop library first with heavy reuse before you draw a single scene.

### Risk 2: scope, and this is the important one

Chapters 0 through 5 as described is a 30 to 45 hour narrative RPG with five distinct settlements, a full trade system, an economy, and a cast of a dozen. Solo, part-time, alongside an engineering degree, that is a four to six year project. Most projects at that scope are abandoned in year two, and the reason is almost never talent.

**Ship Chapter 0 plus Chapter 1 as a complete, standalone, paid game.** Two to four hours, ends on Jack running the shop while Fen watches. It is a whole story with a real arc: a mute kid runs away and finds someone who will teach him. That game is finishable in roughly twelve to eighteen months of consistent part-time work. It builds you an audience, a wishlist, revenue, and, critically, proof to yourself that you finish things.

Chapters 2 to 5 then become a sequel or paid episodes funded by a game that already exists.

### Risk 3: pricing

"A couple of euros" undersells you and actively hurts sales. Sub-5 EUR on Steam signals shovelware and gets you filtered out of exactly the recommendation algorithms you need. A polished three-hour narrative pixel game sits at **8 to 12 EUR**. Budget the **100 USD Steam Direct fee** and expect the store page, trailer and screenshots to take three to four weeks of real work that has nothing to do with the game.

Content rating: the drug delivery arc, the off-screen beating, and the animal deaths put you at PEGI 16 territory. Steam permits all of it, but the store page needs explicit content warnings, particularly for animal harm. Put it in the description, prominently. The people who need that warning are the same people who will love the game, and blindsiding them will cost you reviews.

## 11. Locked decisions

Settled as of this revision. Do not revisit without a reason.

- **Setting:** unplaced. Not Japan, not France, not America. Fictional coastal country, euros as a neutral currency.
- **The impairment:** selective mutism secondary to the household. He can speak physically and has never had a reason safe enough to.
- **Names:** Jacob and Jacqueline, both called Jack. The mother is the only character who ever says the full name, twice in the whole game.
- **Wren:** friend only through Chapter 5. Revisit after Chapter 5 ships, not before.
- **Perspective:** 3/4 top-down, Stardew Valley reference. Not isometric.
- **First release:** Chapters 0, 1 and 2 as a complete standalone game.

## 12. Still open

- Whether Jack's boy and girl portraits share one frame with a swapped head, or are drawn fully separate. Shared frame is roughly a third of the portrait work and stays consistent. Decide before forty portraits exist.
- Whether the four seasonal grave states differ in weather and light only, or whether the marker itself visibly weathers.
- What surname Jack invents on the form in Chapter 3. It should be something the player recognises from Loden, which means it needs to be planted in Chapter 1.
- Which three items the wooden box offers by default, and whether the list is fixed or partly discovered during the montage.
